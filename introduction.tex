\section{Introduction}

Some patterns are hard to turn a blind eye on, especially when they might sway the outcome one way or another. Playing cards is one of my favourite ways to connect with people, and so I decided to explore the maths my favourite card game "Speed". For a while now, I've had a suspicion that the more rounds of a game we played, the more ordered the cards became, however I still wondered if this was in fact true, or if I had simply jumped to logical conclusions.

This investigation will use primary data to assess the orderedness of cards as the rounds progress, and then employ graphing and statistical strategies to analyse if my hypothesis is correct or null and void of validity.

 \subsubsection{Rules}
 
 The aim of the game is to finish the layered cards in front of you. A card can be played if it is either one above or one below (not the same) as the card shown on the playing pile, and suits don't matter.

 Thus, the more similar in number your cards are, the more of them you can play. Each turn, both players draw a card, and play out their own cards onto either pile until neither can play anymore, then another card is drawn and the turns repeat until one player depletes their cards. At this point, either player can select the smallest pile in front of them, slap it, and claim it as part of their cards, while the loser is condemned to the larger pile. These newly claimed piles are added on top of each respective draw pile, without being shuffled, and the players set up another round within the larger game. 
 
\subsection{Definitions}
\begin{itemize}
	\item  Number of rounds played ($a$): the rounds played within one game, with no shuffling between each one
	\item  Cards flipped from the draw pile ($b$): The number of cards flipped, simultaneously, by both players 
	\item  Length of longest chain ($c$): the number of cards that are either one up or one down from the previous card creating a chain, applicable for this specific game 
\end{itemize}
