\section{Introduction}

Some patterns are hard to turn a blind eye on, especially when they might sway the outcome one way or another. While playing my favourite card game "Speed", I noticed that the more rounds of a game we played, the more ordered the cards became, which made me wonder if this was in fact true, or if I had jumped to logical conclusions and if ... Playing cards is one of my favourite ways to connect with people, and so I wanted to explore the maths behind this game and work out if my assumption proved true.

GAME EXPLAINED

 In the game of "Speed", the deck is split evenly in half, and each player sets up their half of the deck, respectively, in a layered formation, as shown below:
 
 Figure 1: first layer - one faced up, four faced down
 
 Figure 2: second layer - one faced up, three faced down
 
 Figure 3: third layer - one faced up, two faced down
 
 Figure 4: fourth layer - one faced up, one faced down
 
 Figure 5: fifth layer - one faced up
 
 The remainder of each half deck is placed in the centre, and now act as draw piles, that are played alongside onto the respective playing pile:
 
 Figure 6: final setup of Speed
 
 The aim of the game is to finish the layered cards in front of you. A card can be played if it is either one above or one below (not the same) the card shown on the playing pile, and suits don't matter. 
 Thus, the more similar in number your cards are, the more of them you can play. Each turn, both players draw a card, and play out their own cards onto either pile until neither can play anymore, then another card is drawn and the turns repeat until one player depletes their cards. At this point, either player can select the smallest pile in front of them, slap it, and claim it as part of their cards, while the loser is condemned to the larger pile
